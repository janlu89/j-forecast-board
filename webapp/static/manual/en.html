For people who are not technical and don’t speak “business jargon”. No formulas: just what you see on the screen, what it means and what you can click.

It works like a weather forecast, but for the company’s numbers. And like the weather, it doesn’t just say “20 degrees tomorrow”: it also says “almost certainly between 17 and 23”. That range is the most useful part.

🎨 The colours: one key for every chart

Every chart always uses the same colours. Once you know them, you can read every page.

White – the real data the model was allowed to see.
Orange – the “test on the past”: a period the model did not see, forecast blind and then compared with reality.
Green – the real future, which nobody knows yet.
Coloured band – the margin. “80%” means: 8 times out of 10 the real value falls inside the band.
Grey or yellow dashed – a “simple rule” (e.g. “same as last week”), for comparison.
Purple – a scenario you made up (“what if we ran a promotion?”).
Red dot – an unusual day, outside the normal range.
Blue – background information: holidays, promotions, opening days…
💡 Move the mouse (or your finger) over a chart and a small box shows the exact numbers for that day. Use the scroll wheel or drag to zoom in.

🎛️ The top bar (always visible)

It is the “remote control”: choose which data to look at, which store and which version of the model. It applies to every page.
  1. The pages (the tabs): Overview, Series, Total… Click to change page. The page you are on is orange. On the right, 📘 Guida opens this guide.
  2. Dataset = which collection of data. There are public examples already (sales of 1,115 Rossmann stores, electricity use, bike rentals, temperatures…), and the data you upload appears here too.
  3. Store / Series = which store (or product, or city). It only appears when the data contains many of them.
  4. Arrows ◀ ▶ = previous / next store, to flip through them one at a time.
  5. Model = which version of the “brain”. The number (5M, 28M, 123M, 209M) is its size: bigger = usually more accurate but slower. The words after the name say how it was used: no covariates = without background information; closed = missing = closed days treated as “empty”, not as “zero sales”. Entries marked not computed have not been calculated yet: choosing one starts the calculation.
  6. Horizon = how many days (or hours, or months) into the future to forecast. Changing it redoes the calculations.
  7. Context = how much of the past the model reads (0 = all of it). Usually there is no need to touch it.
  8. Yellow pill ⏳ = a calculation is running in the background: it shows the percentage and the minutes left. Pages update by themselves when it finishes.

📊 Overview – “does the model work?”

The report card. For every store the last period was hidden, the model forecast it without seeing it, and then we checked how wrong it was. Here you find the overall grade and the comparison with simple methods.

When to use it: first, to get the answer in 30 seconds.

Top: the summary and the 6 key numbers

  1. The summary sentence: which dates the model saw, which period was hidden (Holdout), how far the future forecast goes and which version did best.
  2. Notes on how the data is handled (e.g. gaps in the data, closed days).
  3. Average error (MAPE): how wrong it is on average, as a percentage. 9.98% = on a day worth €1,000 it is off by about €100 on average. Underneath, in green, how much better it is than the best simple rule.
  4. A second way to measure error (RMSPE, the one used in the Kaggle competition): it punishes big mistakes more. Lower = better.
  5. Error on total volume (WAPE): all the errors added up, divided by total sales. It is the “how wrong was I on turnover” view.
  6. How reliable the band is (Coverage of the 80% band): how often the real value fell inside the band. It should be close to 80%: much less = the model is over-confident; much more = it is too cautious.
  7. Error on the total (e.g. the whole chain): when all stores are added up, the errors partly cancel out, so it is lower.
  8. Calculation time on this ordinary PC, without a graphics card.

Middle: the leaderboard

  1. The winner 🏆 (highlighted row). Orange rows are Timestral, the others are the simple rules.
  2. A simple rule, for example Mean weekday × promo = “average of the same weekday, with or without a promotion”. They show whether the model adds value.
  3. The columns are different ways of measuring error: for almost all of them lower = better. Each one is explained in Difficult words.
  4. Green number = the best in that column.
  5. Add to the comparison: click a ▶ button to calculate another version of the model (e.g. the bigger 209M). It runs in the background; the yellow pill at the top shows progress.

Bottom: where it goes wrong

  1. Error by step: how wrong it is forecasting 1 day ahead, 2 days, … up to the last one. Usually, the further ahead, the bigger the error.
  2. Distribution per store: each bar counts how many stores have that error. The further left the orange “mountain” sits, the better.
  3. Error by weekday: for example, Sunday is harder.
  4. Calibration: checks whether the bands are honest. If the orange line sits on the dashed diagonal, “80%” really means 80%.
  5. Error by group (e.g. store type). Choose the grouping from the menu on the right.
  6. Best and worst stores. Click a row to open that store in the Series page.

🏪 Series – one store at a time

The chart of a single store: what happened, what the model had predicted and what it predicts for the future.

When to use it: to show someone a concrete example (“look at store 1”). Change store with the ◀ ▶ arrows at the top.

  1. Show baseline = show the simple rule (grey dashed line) to compare it with the model.
  2. All / Last year = show the whole history or only the last year.
  3. Backtest + future = zoom on the interesting part: the test on the past and the future (recommended view).
  4. Forecast CSV = download the forecast as a file that opens in Excel (date, forecast value, low and high end of the band).
  5. The labels: store number, characteristics (type, assortment, distance to the nearest competitor…), error on this store and reliability of the band.
  6. The key to the chart colours. Click an entry to hide or show it.
  7. Orange zone = the period hidden from the model. White line = what really happened; orange line = what it had predicted. The closer they are, the better.
  8. Green zone = the predicted future, with the dark band (8 times out of 10) and the lighter one (9 times out of 10).
  9. Mini-map: drag its edges to choose the period to look at.
  1. The background information (Covariates) given to the model: school holidays, public holidays, promotions, opening days… These are things known in advance (you already know when you will run a promo or close). Blue bars mark the “yes” days; faded ones are estimated.
  2. This store’s report card: the same measures as the Overview, just for this store.

➕ Total – the whole chain together

The sum of all stores. It answers: “how much will we sell in total?”. It only appears when the data contains several stores.
  1. Two ways to forecast the total: bottom-up (orange/green) = forecast every store and then add them up; direct (purple dashed) = forecast the total directly.
  2. Orange zone = hidden period, to compare with reality (white line).
  3. Leaderboard on the total: who makes the smallest error on the chain total.
💡 In the Rossmann data, adding up the stores (5.6% error) works much better than forecasting the total directly: on Sundays almost every store is closed and the total “jumps”.

⏪ Explore backtest – the time machine

Pick a day in the past and pretend it is “today”: everything after it disappears (from the chart too) and the model forecasts from there. Press ▶ and time moves forward, so you see the forecast change as new data arrives.

When to use it: to show “live” how the model reasons. It is the most impressive page in a presentation.

  1. Frames = how many “todays” to prepare (20, 40, 80). More frames = smoother movement, but a longer calculation.
  2. Look-back = how far back the time travel starts (auto is fine).
  3. Compute = prepare the time machine (a few seconds). Press it once.
  4. Reveal hidden data = also show what really happened after “today” (grey dotted line), to check.
  5. Follow “today” = the chart follows the chosen day instead of showing the whole period.
  6. Speed = playback speed.
  7. ▶ Play / ❚❚ Pause: makes time move.
  8. Slider: drag it to choose the “today” yourself. The date is shown on the right.
  9. Error for each “today”: a tall bar = that time it was more wrong. Click a bar to jump to that day.

⚡ Live forecast – a forecast on the spot

Choose a store and a date: the model calculates straight away (a few seconds) what will happen next. If the date is in the past you also see what really happened and the score.
  1. Forecast from = which day to start from (empty = from the last available day, i.e. the real future).
  2. Forecast = calculate.
  3. End of data = forecast the real future, from the last existing data point.
  4. − 1 horizon = go back one period (e.g. 48 days) and try again: handy for many tests in a row.
  5. Random date = a random date: good for showing it isn’t “rigged” on a hand-picked day.
  6. The result: error against reality, reliability of the band and Bias (+ = forecast too high, − = too low).
  7. White dashed line = the day the forecast starts from. On the right: green = forecast; grey dotted = what really happened.

✂️ Split test – “here are 2 years, guess the other 3”

The toughest test. Take a stretch of history (e.g. 5 years), show the model only part of it (e.g. the first 2) and ask it to forecast all the rest. Then it is compared, year by year and month by month, with reality and with the simple rules.

When to use it: to find out how far ahead you can trust it (1 month? 1 year? 3 years?). It works best with long data: try Germany electricity or Melbourne temperature, or your own.

  1. Data from = where the data to use starts.
  2. Model sees until = up to when the model may look. Everything after this date is hidden.
  3. Forecast until = how far it must forecast.
  4. Run split test = start the test with the chosen dates.
  5. Shortcuts: 5 years: 2 → 3 fills in the dates for you (5 years, 2 seen, 3 to forecast). There are also 1→1, 3→1 and 1 year → 3 months.
  6. Show best simple rule = show/hide the best simple rule (yellow dotted line).
  7. The summary: how much it saw, how much it forecast, its error (red if a simple rule did better) and that of the best rule.
  8. The cut line: on the left (white) what the model saw, on the right the forecast (green) over reality (grey).
  1. Error year by year: does the orange bar (Timestral) grow as it moves away? Then the further ahead it looks, the less precise it is: that is normal.
  2. “How the model did it” in plain words: how much it saw, how much it had to forecast, how it widens the margin over time, and the final verdict.
  3. The leaderboard on the hidden period: who made the smallest error.
  1. Model error month by month: green = under 5%, red = over 15%. “+” = it forecast more than reality, “−” = less.
  2. Simple rule error in the same month, for comparison.
⚠️ An honest result: with German electricity use (a very regular series), 3 years ahead the rule “same day last year” makes a smaller error (2.9%) than Timestral (4.6%). With temperatures and airline passengers Timestral wins. The test exists precisely to find this out on your data.

🎚️ What-if – “what would happen if…?”

Change the information you know in advance (promotions, opening days, holidays, weather forecast) and immediately see how the forecast would change. Green = normal situation, purple = your scenario.

When to use it: “if we run the promo every week, how much more do we sell?”, “if we close the first week, how much do we lose?”.

  1. Ready-made scenarios: one click and it runs (e.g. “promo every weekday”, “no promotions”, “closed the first week”).
  2. What to do with this information: keep = leave it as it is; set to = set a value; shift by = add/subtract (e.g. +5 degrees); scale × = multiply (e.g. ×1.2 = +20%).
  3. The value: for yes/no, 1 = yes and 0 = no.
  4. Which days: from “step” to “step”, counting from the first forecast day (0 = the first day, 7 = one week later).
  5. Run scenario = calculate the scenario with your choices.
  6. Reset = back to the normal situation.
  7. The result in numbers: normal forecast total, total with the scenario and the difference (in value and in %).
  8. Key: green = base, purple = scenario. Under the chart you also see which information you changed.
💡 The model learns the effect of a promo from that store’s past: if promotions used to move sales very little, the scenario will move them very little too.

🚨 Anomalies – the strange days

The model redoes many small forecasts over the recent past and marks with a red dot every day that fell outside the band: breakdowns, storms, data errors, special events.
  1. Window length = length of each small forecast (e.g. 7 days).
  2. Windows = how many to make going backwards (20 × 7 days = about 5 months checked).
  3. Band = how strict to be. 98% = only flags things that are really out of the ordinary; 90% = more sensitive, flags more.
  4. Detect anomalies = start the search.
  5. The summary: how many strange days, and the percentage outside the band compared with what is “normal by chance” (about 2% with the 98% band).
  6. Red dot = an anomalous day; the bigger it is, the stranger the day.
  7. The list, strangest first: date, real value, expected value, the band, and whether it was above ▲ or below ▼.

📤 Your data – trying it with our own numbers

Upload a file exported from Excel and the model analyses it just like the examples. The file never leaves the demo PC.

How to prepare the file (in Excel)

📅
One column with the datee.g. 01/03/2024
🏷️
(Optional) who it isstore, product, city… one row for each, for every day
🔢
The number to forecastsales, units, customers…
➕
(Optional) extra informationpromo yes/no, open yes/no, price…
💾
Save as CSVFile → Save As → “CSV (Comma delimited)”
💡 To try it right away there is a made-up sample file in the project folder: esempio_vendite_negozi.csv (3 stores, 2 years, with promotions). If you want the model to know about future promotions, add rows for the future days at the end with the promo filled in and the sales column empty (the sample already does this for 30 days).
  1. Drag the file here or click to choose it.
  2. Preview: the first rows, to check it is the right file.
  3. Name = the name it will have in the Dataset menu.
  4. Date column = which column is the date (it usually finds it by itself).
  5. Series ID column = which column says who the row belongs to (store, product). Leave “single series” if the file has only one line of numbers.
  6. Forecast this = the number to forecast.
  7. The other columns: ignore = don’t use it; past only = only known afterwards (e.g. customers who came in); known in advance = known beforehand (promo, opening days, list price). category = it is a yes/no or a code, not a quantity.
  8. Frequency = how often there is a data point (daily, weekly, monthly…). auto-detect = it works it out by itself.
  9. Season / horizon = length of the cycle (7 = a week for daily data) and how many steps to forecast. Leaving auto is perfectly fine.
  10. Totals per period = for monthly summaries: sum (sales, units) or average (prices, temperatures).
  11. Calendar features = automatically adds weekday and month as known information. Leave it on.
  12. Public holidays = adds the public holidays of the chosen country (IT = Italy).
  13. Load dataset = load it. The Overview opens: the yellow pill shows the calculation in progress (seconds for a few stores, minutes for hundreds).
⚠️ Uploaded data stays in memory only while the program is running: if the PC restarts, it has to be uploaded again.

📖 Difficult words, explained simply

ForecastA prediction of future numbers.
Holdout / backtestThe period hidden from the model to test it – like a school test where the teacher already knows the answers.
BaselineA simple rule used as a yardstick (e.g. “same as last week”, “same day last year”).
MAPEAverage error as a percentage, day by day. 10% = on average it is off by a tenth. Lower = better.
WAPEError on total volume: how wrong I was overall compared with how much I sold overall. Lower = better.
RMSPELike MAPE but weighs big mistakes more (it is the measure of the Kaggle Rossmann competition).
sMAPEA “symmetric” version of MAPE. Lower = better.
MASEComparison with the rule “repeat the last cycle”: below 1 = better than the rule, above 1 = worse.
MAE / RMSEAverage error in the units of the data (e.g. euros per day). RMSE weighs big mistakes more.
BiasTendency: + = always forecasts a bit too much; − = always a bit too little. Close to 0 = balanced.
Coverage 80% (Cov. 80%)How often the real value fell inside the band. Ideal ≈ 80%.
Width 80%How wide the band is compared with the values: narrow = precise, wide = cautious.
WQLA grade for the whole band, not just the middle line. Lower = better.
CovariatesBackground information given to the model: promos, opening days, holidays, weather…
Known in advanceInformation known beforehand (the promo calendar, planned closures).
Past onlyInformation only known afterwards (e.g. customers who came in yesterday).
Closed = missing / = 0How to treat closed days: as “empty” (recommended) or as “zero sales”.
Bottom-up / directTotal obtained by adding up the forecasts of each store / by forecasting the total directly.
HorizonHow far ahead to forecast.
ContextHow much of the past the model reads.
Zero-shotThe model is not “trained” on your data: it reads it and forecasts straight away, like an expert seeing a chart for the first time.
5M · 28M · 123M · 209MSize of the model (millions of “parameters”). Bigger = usually more accurate, but slower.

🛟 If something goes wrong

The page doesn’t openIs the demo PC on and the program running? On the demo PC, run avvia_ui.ps1.
It asks for a username and passwordThat is normal: the site is protected. If the password is rejected, ask again whoever runs the demo (it is in the file accesso_demo.json on the demo PC).
“Timestral is evaluating…”It is the first calculation for that data: wait, the yellow pill at the top shows the minutes left. Afterwards it is saved and the next time is instant.
A button does nothingCheck whether a red box with an error message appears at the bottom right; it often says what to fix (e.g. “too many steps”).
The 209M model is very slowThat is normal on this PC without a graphics card: for 1,115 stores it takes about 20–25 minutes. For tests, use 28M.
My file isn’t read properlyCheck there is a single header row, the date in a single column and numbers without symbols (no “€”).
My data has disappearedAfter the program restarts, uploaded files must be uploaded again (by design: they are not saved to disk).
